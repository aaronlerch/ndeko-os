# The Voss toolkit

Source: *Never Split the Difference* (Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, 2016). Written 2026-10-10 from working knowledge of the book, not checked line by line against the text. Treat the specific figures (Ackerman percentages, 7-38-55) as the book's heuristics, not research findings.

## The premise

People decide emotionally and justify rationally. The goal isn't winning an argument; it's finding the information the other side holds and making them feel understood enough to share it. Compromise ("splitting the difference") usually means both sides lose: meeting halfway on shoes means one black and one brown.

## Listening tools

| Tool | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tactical empathy** | Showing you understand their position and feelings. It doesn't mean agreeing. | Everything below is in service of this. |
| **Mirror** | Repeat their last one to three words, as a question, then go quiet. They expand. | "...the budget's locked?" |
| **Label** | Name the emotion or position you sense. "It seems / sounds / looks like...", never "I think" or "I hear," which centers you. Then silence. | "It sounds like the timeline is what's really squeezing you." |
| **Accusation audit** | Before they can, list every negative thing they might be thinking about you. Saying it aloud defuses it. | "You're probably going to think this ask is unreasonable, and that we're not valuing what you've built." |
| **Summary → "That's right"** | Restate their world so well they say "that's right." That's genuine agreement. "You're right" means they want you to stop talking. | |
| **Dynamic silence** | After a label or mirror, stop. The pause does the work. | |
| **Late-night FM DJ voice** | Slow, calm, downward inflection for things that aren't up for debate. Playful/positive voice by default. | Spoken channels only. |

## Steering tools

- **Calibrated questions.** Open "what" and "how" questions that make the counterpart solve your problem and give them the illusion of control. "How am I supposed to do that?" is the gentlest no available. "What about this is important to you?" "How would you like me to proceed?" "What's the biggest challenge you face here?"
- **No-oriented questions.** "No" feels safe and protective, so people engage. "Is it a ridiculous idea to...?" "Have you given up on this project?" (to restart a stalled thread; that one is blunt, so use it sparingly). Pushing for "yes" early produces counterfeit yeses.
- **Three kinds of yes.** Counterfeit (to escape), confirmation (reflexive), commitment (real). Only the last counts. Test it with the Rule of Three: get agreement three times in different forms (a label, a summary, a "how will we implement this?").
- **"How" protects implementation.** "How will we know we're on track?" and "How does this affect the rest of your team?" surface the people who aren't at the table but can kill the deal.
- **The word "fair"** is an emotional grenade. Defend against it ("I want you to feel I'm treating you fairly at all times, so stop me if...") and never use it as a weapon.
- **Pinocchio effect.** Liars use more words and more third person. Watch for it in a counterpart's message.

## Bargaining tools

- **Let them anchor first** when you don't know the market. When you must go first, use a range or a precise number.
- **Loss aversion.** People take more risk to avoid a loss than to secure a gain. Frame what they lose by not agreeing.
- **Deadlines are mostly arbitrary.** Theirs and yours.
- **Ackerman model.** Set a target. Open at 65% of it, then move to 85%, 95%, and 100%, each concession smaller than the last, with empathy and "how am I supposed to do that?" between them. The final number is precise and non-round (37,893, not 38,000), and it comes with a non-monetary item to show you're at the limit.
  - **When prices are public** (used cars, listed goods, posted rates), a 65% opener reads as unserious and ends the conversation. Open where the comparable listings defend it, step up in shrinking increments (for example +500, +300, +150), and keep the precise final number plus the non-monetary item. This adaptation is mine, not the book's.
- **Non-monetary currency.** What do they value that's cheap for you to give? Scope, timing, references, case studies, terms.

## Black swans

Unknown facts that, once known, change the whole negotiation: a constraint they haven't said, a person behind them, a reason they're acting "irrationally." Behavior that looks crazy usually means missing information, a hidden constraint, or a hidden interest. Hunt for these with labels and calibrated questions, ideally face to face or by voice.

## Counterpart types

| Type | Wants | Silence means | Mistake against them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analyst** | Data, preparation, no surprises | They're thinking | Rushing them; surprising them; emotional appeals |
| **Accommodator** | Relationship, time together | They're angry | Treating friendliness as agreement; it may be a counterfeit yes |
| **Assertive** | Being heard, winning, speed | Their chance to talk more | Arguing before you've summarized; they won't listen until heard |

Your own type predicts your blind spot. The golden rule here is "treat them how *they* want to be treated," not how you do.

## One-sheet (prep structure)

1. **Goal**: the best realistic outcome, written down, plus the walk-away.
2. **Summary**: the situation in a few sentences they would agree to ("that's right").
3. **Labels and accusation audit**: three to five of each, drafted ahead of time.
4. **Calibrated questions**: three to five that reveal value, surface deal-killers, and set up "how am I supposed to do that?"
5. **Non-cash offers**: what they'd value that's cheap to give.
6. **Ackerman plan**: if the counterpart is in bounds for it.
